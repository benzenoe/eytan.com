Hydrogen Energy: Powering a Global Green Revolution

July 10, 2025 • Eytan Benzeno

Hydrogen Energy: Powering a Global Green Revolution

From Grey to Green – The Spectrum of Hydrogen Types

Hydrogen comes in many colors (figuratively) depending on how it’s produced. Traditionally most hydrogen has been grey, made from natural gas without capturing emissions, but newer low-carbon types are rising. The key varieties are:

Green Hydrogen

Produced by splitting water (electrolysis) using renewable electricity (solar, wind, etc.), yielding hydrogen and oxygen with zero CO₂ emissions. Green hydrogen is currently a small share of supply (it’s still costly), but like solar and wind power, its cost is expected to fall sharply with scale.

Blue Hydrogen

Made from fossil fuels (usually natural gas) via steam methane reforming, paired with carbon capture to trap the CO₂ byproduct. Blue hydrogen is considered “low-carbon” – it still generates CO₂ during production, but captures most of it for storage instead of releasing it. It’s a bridge solution leveraging existing natural gas resources while cutting most emissions.

White Hydrogen

Refers to naturally occurring hydrogen found in underground geological formations. This hydrogen is generated by natural geochemical reactions and exists in certain rock reservoirs. It requires no energy-intensive production – it “comes ready-made” – which means if tapped, it could be extremely cheap (potentially under $1 per kg). However, white hydrogen is a nascent field: only experimental projects (e.g. a pilot well in Mali powering a village) exist so far, and most potential reserves remain unexplored.

Each type has a different carbon footprint and production method, but all hydrogen fuel is emission-free at point of use. Whether burned or used in a fuel cell, hydrogen recombines with oxygen to produce only water vapor. This makes hydrogen an attractive clean energy carrier – especially when produced in a low-carbon way (green, blue, or possibly white) instead of the traditional high-emission methods.

Safety Matters: Hydrogen’s Safety Profile

As a fuel, hydrogen must be handled with care – but its risks are well-understood and manageable with proper engineering. In fact, hydrogen has some safety advantages over conventional fuels. Hydrogen gas is non-toxic and, being the lightest element, disperses upwards and dilutes quickly if leaked.

Hydrogen is highly flammable over a wide range of concentrations in air and has a low ignition energy. For this reason, hydrogen systems require vigilant leak detection, ventilation, and ignition source control. Hydrogen flames burn nearly invisible, so special flame sensors are used. Materials selection is also crucial: hydrogen can embrittle metals, requiring specialized alloys and composites.

Industry has decades of experience handling hydrogen in refineries and chemical plants. Modern hydrogen tanks are carbon-fiber composites tested against impact and penetration. Fueling stations use automated shut-offs and sensors. Studies and real-world data show hydrogen can be as safe as gasoline or natural gas when proper protocols are followed. As hydrogen adoption grows, safety standards continue to evolve alongside deployment.

Driving the Future: Hydrogen in Transport

Hydrogen is expanding rapidly in the transport sector, particularly through fuel cell electric vehicles (FCEVs). These vehicles generate electricity onboard using hydrogen and emit only water. Major models include the Toyota Mirai, Hyundai Nexo, and Honda Clarity.

Over 90,000 FCEVs have been sold globally as of 2024. Asia-Pacific leads adoption, with Japan, South Korea, and China deploying vehicles and hydrogen refueling stations at scale. Japan and South Korea view hydrogen mobility as a strategic pillar of energy security and industrial competitiveness. South Korea aims for 6.2 million hydrogen vehicles by 2040.

Hydrogen’s advantages include 3–5 minute refueling times and high energy density, making it ideal for heavy-duty transport such as trucks, buses, trains, ships, and potentially aircraft. Germany operates hydrogen trains on non-electrified rail lines. Hyundai and Toyota are deploying hydrogen trucks. Airbus has announced plans for a hydrogen-powered aircraft by 2035.

Hydrogen complements battery EVs, particularly where weight, range, and downtime matter. As green hydrogen costs fall, hydrogen mobility is expected to expand rapidly.

Fueling Industry: Hydrogen’s Role in Industrial Decarbonization

The most transformative impact of hydrogen may occur in heavy industry—steel, cement, chemicals, and refining.

Steel

Traditional steelmaking relies on coal and emits vast CO₂. Hydrogen can replace coal as the reducing agent. In 2021, Sweden’s HYBRIT project produced the world’s first fossil-free steel, delivering it to Volvo. Commercial-scale production is planned by 2026. Similar projects are underway across Europe, North America, and Asia.

Chemicals and Fertilizers

Hydrogen is a core feedstock for ammonia and refining. Transitioning to green hydrogen enables green ammonia and low-carbon fertilizers. Companies such as Yara and CF Industries are investing heavily in electrolyzers.

High-Temperature Heat

Hydrogen can provide industrial heat where electrification is difficult. Pilot projects in cement and glass production have shown successful hydrogen combustion.

The IEA identifies hydrogen as critical for decarbonizing hard-to-abate sectors. Governments are supporting early adoption through subsidies, contracts-for-difference, and carbon pricing. Hydrogen is enabling a green industrial revolution without sacrificing productivity.

Powering the Grid: Hydrogen for Energy Storage and Electricity

Hydrogen enables long-duration energy storage, absorbing excess renewable power and delivering it back when needed. This makes hydrogen a seasonal, grid-scale energy buffer.

In Utah, a green hydrogen project will store 150,000 MWh per cavern in underground salt formations. Gas turbines at the Intermountain Power Project will transition to 100% hydrogen by 2045, providing dispatchable, carbon-free power.

Manufacturers such as GE, Siemens, and Mitsubishi are producing hydrogen-ready turbines. France’s HYFLEXPOWER project demonstrated 100% hydrogen power generation. South Korea targets 15 GW of fuel cell power capacity by 2040.

Hydrogen enables grids with high renewable penetration while preserving reliability—something batteries alone cannot economically achieve at scale.

Investment Boom and Market Trends

Hydrogen investment has surged dramatically. As of 2025:

  • $110+ billion committed to low-carbon hydrogen projects
  • Over 500 projects at final investment decision or construction
  • More than 1,700 projects announced globally
  • Estimated 9–14 million tonnes of clean hydrogen capacity by 2030

The Hydrogen Council has grown from 13 companies in 2017 to 140+ global members, including Shell, BP, TotalEnergies, Toyota, Hyundai, Linde, and Air Liquide.

Examples:

  • Shell plans to invest up to $1 billion annually in hydrogen and CCS (2024–2025)
  • Europe’s largest electrolyzer (100 MW) is under construction in Germany
  • China manufactures 60% of global electrolyzer capacity
  • The U.S. offers up to $3/kg clean hydrogen tax credits

While demand must still scale to match supply, project implementation rates have doubled in the past year, signaling rapid market maturation.

Global Momentum: Policies and Initiatives Worldwide

Europe

The EU targets 20 million tonnes of renewable hydrogen by 2030 (10 Mt domestic, 10 Mt imports). Initiatives include IPCEI funding, a Hydrogen Bank, and a planned 11,600 km hydrogen pipeline network. Germany, France, and the Netherlands are leading implementation.

North America

The U.S. has committed $8 billion to seven regional hydrogen hubs, expected to unlock $40+ billion in private investment and produce 3 million tonnes annually. The Inflation Reduction Act’s hydrogen tax credit has reshaped global project economics.

Asia-Pacific

  • Japan: Targets 12 million tonnes of hydrogen use by 2040, with ~$110 billion in investment.
  • South Korea: Aims for 6 million vehicles and 15 GW of fuel cells by 2040.
  • China: World leader in electrolyzer manufacturing, hydrogen buses, and large-scale green hydrogen projects.

Australia, Chile, Saudi Arabia, and the UAE are positioning as hydrogen exporters, forming the backbone of a future global hydrogen trade.

Environmental and Economic Advantages

Hydrogen enables:

  • Zero-emission transport and industry
  • Deep decarbonization of hard-to-abate sectors
  • Renewable energy storage at massive scale
  • Energy security and diversification
  • Industrial revitalization and job creation

Analysts estimate the hydrogen economy could exceed $2.5 trillion by 2050. CEOs across energy, industry, and transport report increasing investment appetite despite macroeconomic uncertainty.

Conclusion: The Hydrogen Horizon

Hydrogen is no longer theoretical—it is being built now. From green steel and fuel-cell transport to grid-scale energy storage, hydrogen is becoming the backbone of a net-zero economy.

It offers a rare alignment: climate progress, industrial competitiveness, and economic growth. While challenges remain—cost, infrastructure, coordination—the momentum is unmistakable.

Hydrogen allows us to fuel the world without burning the planet. Invisible, flexible, and powerful, it is poised to become one of the defining energy pillars of the 21st century.
